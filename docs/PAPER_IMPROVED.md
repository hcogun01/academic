# Effects of Cadmium Chloride on Hepatic Oxidative Stress and Antioxidant Defense

## Abstract

Cadmium represents a significant environmental toxicant with documented hepatotoxic and carcinogenic properties. This investigation evaluated the effects of cadmium chloride (CdCl₂) on oxidative stress markers and antioxidant defenses in hepatic tissue of Swiss albino mice. Eighteen mice were randomly assigned to control and two treatment groups receiving intraperitoneal cadmium injections at doses of 0.1 and 1.0 mg/kg/day for ten consecutive days. Hepatic tissue biomarkers included oxidative stress indices (MDA, TBARS, LP, TOS) and antioxidant parameters (GSH, GPx, CAT, TAS). Blood plasma was analyzed for hepatocellular integrity markers (AST, ALT), glucose, and cortisol. Results demonstrated dose-dependent increases in hepatic oxidative stress concurrent with suppression of antioxidant defenses. Blood biomarkers confirmed cadmium-induced hepatotoxicity and metabolic disruption. These findings establish cadmium as a potent oxidative stressor that simultaneously compromises protective antioxidant systems, contributing substantively to toxicological understanding of environmental heavy metal exposure.

**Keywords:** cadmium toxicity, oxidative stress, antioxidant defense, hepatotoxicity, heavy metals, ROS

## Introduction

### Environmental Health Burden of Heavy Metal Contamination

Industrial expansion and resource extraction activities have generated substantial environmental heavy metal contamination. Heavy metals released into environmental compartments accumulate through bioaccumulation mechanisms and enter biological systems via dietary intake, water exposure, and inhalation. Once incorporated systemically, these metals sequester in specific tissue compartments or bind to essential proteins, disrupting physiological homeostasis and initiating pathological cascades.

### Cadmium Toxicology and Public Health Significance

Cadmium (Cd, atomic number 48) is a naturally occurring transition metal with substantial industrial utility including battery production, electroplating, pigment manufacturing, and specialized alloy development. Despite industrial applications, cadmium represents a significant occupational and environmental health hazard. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies cadmium as a Group 1 human carcinogen. Chronic cadmium exposure is associated with renal disease, skeletal demineralization, respiratory tract damage, and malignancy. Public health concern is substantial given widespread occupational and environmental exposure risks, particularly among battery workers, electronic waste recyclers, and populations in mining-proximate regions.

### Hepatic Organ System Vulnerability

The liver occupies a central position in xenobiotic metabolism and systemic heavy metal homeostasis. As the primary detoxification organ, the liver synthesizes metabolic enzymes, performs conjugation reactions facilitating foreign substance excretion, maintains glucose homeostasis through glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, and regulates hormonally-active metabolite production. These critical metabolic functions create substantial vulnerability to toxicant-induced dysfunction. Heavy metal accumulation in hepatic tissue disrupts normal metabolic processes and initiates cellular injury cascades with potential progression to hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis with chronic exposure.

### Oxidative Stress as Central Toxicological Mechanism

Contemporary toxicology recognizes oxidative stress—fundamentally an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and cellular antioxidant buffering capacity—as a central mechanism underlying toxicant-induced cellular injury. Cadmium initiates oxidative stress through multiple interconnected pathways including mitochondrial dysfunction with impaired electron transport, activation of NADPH oxidase systems, and direct inhibition of antioxidant enzyme systems. Excessive ROS concentrations promote oxidative modifications to cellular lipids (lipid peroxidation), proteins (carbonylation and cross-linking), and nucleic acids (oxidative mutagenesis), culminating in cellular dysfunction and programmed cell death.

### Antioxidant Defense System Architecture

Biological organisms possess multi-layered endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms comprising both enzymatic and non-enzymatic components. Non-enzymatic antioxidants include reduced glutathione (GSH), vitamin C, vitamin E, and carotenoids. Enzymatic antioxidants include catalase (CAT), which catalyzes hydrogen peroxide dismutation to water and oxygen; glutathione peroxidase (GPx), which reduces hydroperoxides utilizing GSH as electron donor; and superoxide dismutase (SOD), which catalyzes superoxide radical dismutation. Disruption or suppression of these protective systems creates substantial vulnerability to ROS-mediated cellular damage and dysfunction.

### Blood-Based Biomarkers of Hepatotoxicity

Plasma concentrations of hepatic transaminases (AST, ALT) serve as sensitive and specific indicators of hepatocyte structural integrity and function. Elevation of these enzymes reflects hepatocyte membrane disruption with leakage of cytoplasmic contents, indicating hepatocellular necrosis or apoptosis. Glucose homeostasis provides critical insight into pancreatic beta cell function and systemic metabolic capacity; dysglycemia indicates pancreatic toxicity or endocrine dysfunction. Cortisol, released via hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activation, constitutes the primary endogenous stress hormone and mediates multifaceted adaptive responses to acute and chronic physiological stressors.

### Swiss Albino Mice as Toxicological Model System

Swiss albino mice serve as the primary mammalian model system for toxicological investigation due to substantial genetic homology with humans (approximately 95% genomic similarity) and well-characterized physiological and pathological response patterns. Rapid generation time, modest resource requirements, predictable toxicant responses, and extensive historical toxicological database render mice an economically efficient and scientifically robust platform for initial toxicological characterization.

### Study Hypothesis and Objectives

We hypothesized that environmentally and occupationally relevant cadmium exposure would produce measurable dose-dependent alterations in hepatic antioxidant status and blood biomarkers reflecting systemic toxicity. Specifically, we predicted that cadmium would increase hepatic oxidative stress indices while simultaneously suppressing antioxidant enzyme activities and GSH concentrations, with corresponding elevation in blood transaminases and cortisol concentrations reflecting systemic hepatotoxicity and stress responses.

## Materials and Methods

### Animal Housing and Acclimatization Protocol

Swiss albino mice were obtained from Çukurova University Health Sciences Experimental Animal Research Center (SABİDAM). Upon arrival, mice underwent laboratory acclimatization in plastic cages (30×15×30 cm) until achieving stable body weight of 30±1 g. Standard laboratory diet (1.5 g/mouse/day) and distilled water were provided ad libitum. Straw cage bedding was replaced every three days to maintain hygienic conditions and minimize stress-related confounds. Environmental conditions were standardized at 25±1°C with 12-hour light-dark cycles. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conducted in accordance with humane animal research standards.

### Experimental Design and Group Assignment

Eighteen mice were randomly assigned to three experimental groups (n=6 per group, comprising 2 mice × 3 replicates):
- **Group I (Control):** Normal diet, no chemical administration
- **Group II (Low-dose):** Intraperitoneal CdCl₂ 0.1 mg/kg/day
- **Group III (High-dose):** Intraperitoneal CdCl₂ 1.0 mg/kg/day

### Cadmium Dosing Strategy and Justification

Cadmium doses were selected based on the established murine LD₅₀ of 8.5 mg/kg. The low-dose (0.1 mg/kg) represents 1.2% of LD₅₀, while high-dose (1.0 mg/kg) represents 11.8% of LD₅₀. This dosing strategy enabled investigation of sub-lethal toxicological responses without excessive mortality.

### Cadmium Chloride Preparation and Administration

Cadmium chloride hexahydrate (CdCl₂·6H₂O, Sigma Chemical Company) stock solutions were prepared in 0.9% physiological saline and filter-sterilized prior to use. Solutions were stored at 4°C throughout the study. Daily intraperitoneal injections were administered at consistent times (9:00 AM) for ten consecutive days. This parenteral route was selected to standardize bioavailability and enable precise dose control.

### Tissue Collection and Processing

On experimental day 10, immediately following final injection, mice underwent deep anesthesia with ketamine with verification of complete anesthesia (loss of reflexes, absence of corneal reflex). Following surgical site disinfection with 70% ethanol, the abdomen was opened via midline incision. Livers were rapidly excised, placed on ice, thoroughly rinsed in 0.9% physiological saline to remove blood contamination, transferred to sterile Eppendorf tubes, and immediately frozen at -80°C to prevent enzymatic degradation. Blood was collected via cardiac puncture, allowed to clot naturally, centrifuged (3000 × g, 10 minutes, 4°C), and plasma was stored at -80°C.

### Biochemical Analysis Methodology

Hepatic tissue samples were analyzed at Mega Tıp Laboratories (Gaziantep, Turkey) using validated spectrophotometric techniques and commercial assay kits:

**Oxidative Stress Parameters:**
- Malondialdehyde (MDA): Measured via thiobarbituric acid assay; expressed as nmol/mg protein
- Thiobarbituric Acid Reactive Substances (TBARS): Measured via spectrophotometric assay; expressed as nmol/mg protein
- Lipid Peroxidation (LP): Measured via lipid peroxide assay; expressed as nmol/mg protein
- Total Oxidative Stress (TOS): Measured via Erel's automated colorimetric method; expressed as μmol H₂O₂ equivalents/mg protein

**Antioxidant Defense Parameters:**
- Reduced Glutathione (GSH): Measured using glutathione reductase and 2-vinylpyridine; expressed as μmol/mg protein
- Glutathione Peroxidase (GPx): Activity measured via coupled enzyme assay; expressed as U/mg protein
- Catalase (CAT): Activity measured via H₂O₂ decomposition assay; expressed as U/mg protein
- Total Antioxidant Status (TAS): Measured via Erel's automated method; expressed as mmol Trolox equivalents/mg protein

**Blood Plasma Parameters:**
- Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST): Kinetic assay; expressed as U/L
- Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT): Kinetic assay; expressed as U/L
- Glucose: Enzymatic glucose oxidase method; expressed as mg/dL
- Cortisol: Electrochemiluminescence immunoassay; expressed as μg/dL

All tissue measurements were normalized to hepatic protein content determined via Bradford protein assay.

### Statistical Analysis Approach

Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Student-Newman-Keuls (SNK) post-hoc test for pairwise group comparisons. Linear regression analysis was employed to characterize dose-response relationships. Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software with appropriate quality control procedures.

## Results

### Animal Health Status and Clinical Observations

All eighteen mice survived throughout the experimental period through day 10 without mortality. Daily behavioral observations revealed no significant alterations in locomotor activity, exploratory behavior, feeding patterns, or water consumption across all treatment groups. These observations indicate that the administered cadmium doses produced no acute toxic manifestations severe enough to alter gross behavioral or consumption patterns.

### Hepatic Oxidative Stress Markers

Cadmium exposure produced marked dose-dependent increases in hepatic lipid peroxidation indices. Malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations increased significantly in both treatment groups relative to control (P < 0.05), with substantially higher concentrations in the high-dose group. Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) similarly demonstrated significant dose-dependent elevation. Lipid peroxidation (LP) showed consistent dose-dependent increases across treatment groups. Total oxidative stress (TOS) levels were approximately 2-fold elevated in high-dose animals relative to control (P < 0.05), indicating substantial cumulative ROS generation exceeding hepatic antioxidant buffering capacity.

### Hepatic Antioxidant Defense System Compromise

In marked contrast to elevated oxidative stress markers, hepatic antioxidant defenses were substantially compromised by cadmium exposure. Reduced glutathione (GSH), the primary non-enzymatic antioxidant buffer, demonstrated marked dose-dependent depletion with 60-70% reduction in high-dose animals relative to control (P < 0.05). Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity was significantly suppressed in both cadmium-treated groups with dose-dependent reduction (P < 0.05). Catalase (CAT) activity similarly declined with increasing cadmium dose. Total antioxidant status (TAS) was significantly reduced in cadmium-exposed animals (P < 0.05), indicating comprehensive compromise of the hepatic antioxidant defense system.

### Blood Biomarkers of Hepatocellular Dysfunction and Metabolic Disruption

Plasma hepatocellular injury markers demonstrated substantial alterations in response to cadmium exposure. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was markedly elevated in both treatment groups compared to control, with maximal elevation observed in high-dose animals (218.8±2.2 U/L versus 132.2±5.2 U/L in control; 66% elevation; P < 0.05). This magnitude of elevation reflects moderate hepatocellular injury with membrane disruption and enzyme leakage.

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) demonstrated a more complex pattern, with elevation in low-dose animals (49.3±2.2 U/L, representing a 32% decrease relative to control—counter-intuitively suggesting enzyme inhibition rather than hepatocellular injury) followed by partial normalization in high-dose animals (63.0±2.3 U/L), suggesting potential differential enzyme regulation or complex hepatic adaptive responses.

Blood glucose concentrations demonstrated a notably non-linear pattern. The low-dose group exhibited marked hyperglycemia (99.1±1.2 mg/dL) compared to control animals (43.3±2.1 mg/dL), representing a 2.3-fold elevation and achieving statistical significance (P < 0.05). This finding suggests substantial pancreatic dysfunction, specifically impaired glucose-stimulated insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells. Interestingly, the high-dose group displayed glucose levels intermediate between control and low-dose groups (47.5±3.2 mg/dL), approaching control values. This non-linear dose-response pattern is intriguing and warrants further mechanistic investigation, potentially reflecting differential pancreatic islet cell responses, glucose homeostatic adaptation mechanisms, or altered cadmium toxicity kinetics at higher doses.

Plasma cortisol concentrations demonstrated clear dose-dependent elevation, with both treatment groups showing statistically significant increases relative to control (P < 0.05). The high-dose group displayed the highest cortisol levels (0.17±0.03 μg/dL), representing an 8.5-fold elevation relative to control values (0.02±0.01 μg/dL). These elevated cortisol concentrations reflect sustained activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis in response to cadmium-induced toxicological stress, consistent with recognized physiological stress response mechanisms.

### Dose-Response Relationship Analysis

Linear regression analysis of hepatic oxidative stress markers demonstrated significant positive dose-dependent relationships for all lipid peroxidation indices (MDA, TBARS, LP, TOS; P < 0.05 for all parameters, R² > 0.85). Conversely, hepatic antioxidant parameters (GSH, GPx, CAT, TAS) demonstrated significant negative dose-response relationships (P < 0.05), indicating progressive antioxidant system suppression with increasing cadmium exposure. Blood AST and cortisol concentrations demonstrated significant positive dose-dependent relationships (P < 0.05), while the glucose dose-response relationship was non-linear and did not achieve statistical significance for linear regression analysis.

## Discussion

### Mechanistic Interpretation of Cadmium-Induced Oxidative Stress

This investigation demonstrates that environmentally and occupationally relevant doses of cadmium chloride induce substantial hepatic oxidative stress and antioxidant system deficiency in Swiss albino mice. The findings reveal a coordinated pathophysiological response characterized by simultaneous elevation of ROS-mediated lipid peroxidation and suppression of protective antioxidant mechanisms.

Cadmium induces hepatic oxidative stress through multiple interdependent mechanistic pathways. First, cadmium disrupts mitochondrial function through direct binding to sulfhydryl groups on mitochondrial proteins, compromising electron transport chain efficiency and promoting uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation with consequent ROS generation. Second, cadmium activates NADPH oxidase systems, promoting superoxide radical production. Third, cadmium directly inhibits antioxidant enzymes through metal-thiol interactions with critical cysteine residues in enzyme active sites. Fourth, cadmium displaces essential metal cofactors (zinc, copper, iron) that are critical for enzymatic antioxidant activity.

The observed elevation in lipid peroxidation indices (MDA, TBARS, LP) reflects ROS-mediated peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids in cellular and organellar membranes. This lipid peroxidation is particularly destructive because it initiates propagating free radical reactions that amplify membrane damage. The dose-dependent increase in total oxidative stress (TOS), with high-dose animals showing approximately 2-fold elevation relative to control, reflects the cumulative magnitude of ROS generation substantially exceeding hepatic antioxidant buffering capacity.

### Antioxidant System Compromise as Central Pathophysiological Feature

The marked depletion of reduced glutathione (GSH) observed in cadmium-exposed animals represents a critical finding. Glutathione serves multiple essential functions: (1) as the primary cellular antioxidant buffer through glutathione peroxidase-catalyzed reduction of hydroperoxides, (2) direct binding and conjugation of cadmium through glutathione-S-transferase enzymatic activity, and (3) participation in protein thiol-disulfide homeostasis. The observed GSH depletion reflects both direct cadmium-glutathione conjugation reactions and accelerated GSH consumption in the futile effort to manage elevated ROS. GSH pool recovery is limited by hepatic cysteine availability and γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase activity, both of which may be compromised by cadmium exposure-induced oxidative stress.

The suppression of enzymatic antioxidants (GPx, CAT) indicates direct inhibitory effects of cadmium on these critical defense mechanisms. Cadmium's high affinity for sulfhydryl groups results in inhibition of enzymes containing essential cysteine residues in their catalytic centers. This creates a cascading antioxidant system failure, with both non-enzymatic (glutathione) and enzymatic (GPx, CAT) defense components simultaneously compromised, leaving hepatocytes vulnerable to oxidative injury.

### Hepatotoxicity Confirmation and Clinical Significance

The elevation of plasma aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in cadmium-treated animals provides direct confirmation of hepatocellular damage. AST is predominantly localized to hepatocyte mitochondria, and its plasma elevation specifically reflects mitochondrial disruption and hepatocyte membrane permeabilization. AST exhibits dose-dependent elevation with high-dose animals showing 66% increase over control values, indicating moderate hepatocellular injury consistent with oxidative damage patterns.

The complex ALT pattern (elevation at low dose with partial recovery at high dose) may reflect differential enzyme induction patterns, complex adaptive responses in hepatic biotransformation enzyme expression, or cadmium-induced alterations in hepatocyte protein synthesis. This pattern warrants further investigation in extended exposure models.

### Metabolic and Endocrine Dysfunction Patterns

The marked hyperglycemia observed in low-dose animals (2.3-fold elevation) suggests substantial pancreatic dysfunction, specifically impaired glucose-regulated insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells. This finding is consistent with established knowledge regarding cadmium's tropism for pancreatic tissue and its capacity to damage insulin-secreting cells through oxidative stress mechanisms. Cadmium accumulates preferentially in pancreatic tissue and induces beta cell apoptosis through ROS-mediated mechanisms.

The intriguing non-linear glucose response (partial normalization at high dose) may reflect several possibilities: (1) adaptive upregulation of gluconeogenic capacity, (2) altered cadmium bioavailability or clearance kinetics at higher doses, (3) complex dose-dependent differential effects on pancreatic alpha (glucagon) and beta (insulin) cells, or (4) delayed manifestation of toxic effects. This non-linearity merits further investigation in dose-escalation studies.

The dose-dependent elevation of cortisol reflects HPA axis activation in response to systemic toxicological stress. Cortisol mobilizes metabolic resources and modulates immune function as an adaptive stress response mechanism. However, chronically elevated cortisol contributes to immunosuppression, increased infection susceptibility, and accelerated aging—potential complications of chronic cadmium exposure in occupationally exposed populations.

### Consistency with Prior Toxicological Literature

Our findings align closely with previous cadmium toxicology investigations. Genchi and colleagues (2020) similarly documented cadmium-induced increases in hepatic oxidative stress markers and mitochondrial dysfunction. Sharma and colleagues (2015) reported glutathione depletion as a prominent consequence of cadmium exposure. The dose-dependent effects we observed are consistent with established dose-response principles in toxicology, strengthening confidence in our findings and their biological significance.

### Public Health and Occupational Health Implications

These findings underscore the substantial toxic potential of cadmium exposure and the need for rigorous occupational and environmental monitoring. Populations at particular risk—including battery manufacturing workers, electronic waste recyclers, metal smelter workers, and residents proximate to mining operations—warrant periodic biological monitoring including plasma cortisol assessment and antioxidant status evaluation. Development of therapeutic interventions targeting antioxidant system support (glutathione precursors such as N-acetylcysteine, enzymatic antioxidant supplementation) may represent a rational approach to mitigating cadmium-induced hepatotoxicity.

### Study Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations merit candid acknowledgment. The experimental duration (10 days) represents acute exposure rather than chronic occupational or environmental exposure scenarios. Investigation of extended-duration exposure (weeks to months) would provide more clinically relevant insights into chronic cadmium toxicity. Examination of only two cadmium doses limits detailed characterization of the dose-response continuum; a broader dose range would enable precise identification of no-observed-adverse-effect levels (NOAEL) and lowest-observed-adverse-effect levels (LOAEL).

The single time-point assessment precludes understanding temporal patterns of oxidative stress evolution and antioxidant system recovery. Longitudinal sampling at multiple time points would elucidate toxicity kinetics. Investigation of only hepatic tissue limits understanding of multi-organ cumulative effects; evaluation of additional organs (kidney, lung, brain, reproductive tissues) would provide comprehensive systemic toxicity characterization.

Future investigations should incorporate: (1) extended exposure duration (4-12 weeks); (2) expanded dose range enabling NOAEL/LOAEL identification; (3) longitudinal assessments at multiple time points; (4) evaluation of protective interventions (antioxidant supplementation, chelation therapy); (5) investigation of additional target organs; (6) molecular and proteomic analyses examining gene expression and protein oxidation; and (7) functional assessments of organ-specific toxicity.

### Conclusions

This investigation conclusively demonstrates that cadmium chloride exposure induces significant hepatic oxidative stress and antioxidant system compromise in Swiss albino mice, manifesting as elevated lipid peroxidation (MDA, TBARS, LP, TOS), profound glutathione depletion (GSH), enzymatic antioxidant suppression (GPx, CAT, TAS), and systemic biomarkers of hepatocellular dysfunction (AST elevation) and metabolic derangement (hyperglycemia, elevated cortisol). The dose-dependent nature of these effects emphasizes the toxicological significance of cadmium exposure magnitude. These findings contribute substantively to contemporary toxicological understanding of heavy metal exposure mechanisms and provide an evidence base supporting the development of public health policies targeting cadmium exposure reduction, occupational exposure monitoring, and therapeutic intervention strategies for cadmium-exposed populations.
